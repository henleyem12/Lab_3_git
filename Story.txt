The rain in Neon Burrow didn’t wash away the scum; it just made it slick.

I’d been awake for seventy-two hours, long enough for the streetlamps to bleed into long, weeping yellow streaks. My coat was damp, my whiskers twitched with a dull ache, and my tail hadn't wagged since '42. When you’re a basset hound in a city full of snakes and stray cats, you learn to keep your nose to the asphalt and your expectations lower than a sewer grate.

The office smelled like wet fur and cold chicory. I was staring at a grease stain on my mahogany desk when the brass doorknob turned.

In slunk Trixie—a Persian with fur the color of milk and eyes like two freshly minted copper pennies. She didn't belong in a dive like this, which meant she had cash, trouble, or a lethal mix of both.

" You Jack?" her voice was a low purr that rasped like sandpaper on velvet.

"Depends on who's asking," I growled, resting my chin on my paws. My eyelids felt like lead weights wrapped in sandpaper. "And whether you brought a case that pays better than kibble."

She didn't waste time. She dropped a silver whistle onto my blotter. It didn't make a sound, but my ears flattened against my skull just looking at it.

"The Syndicate snatched the Alpaca's diamonds," she whispered, leaning over the desk. "The exchange is at the old meatpacking plant. Midnight."

"I don't do cat burglaries, doll. My legs are three inches long and my brain is a fog machine."

"They took your squeaky bone, Jack. The red one."

A cold spark ignited in the ash heap of my chest. That squeaker was personal.

Ten minutes later, I was skulking through the alley behind the plant. My scent tracking was shot—everything smelled like wet trash and rusty iron—but I relied on instinct. I slipped through a broken grate, my drooping jowls practically dragging across the concrete.

Inside, two Dobermans in trench coats were guarding a wooden crate. One was tossing *it* in the air. The red rubber glinted in the dim moonlight.

I didn't bark. Barks are for amateurs. I just knocked over a stack of empty tin cans, let out a mournful howl that echoed like a graveyard ghost, and vanished into the shadows. The guards panicked, bolting into the dark after a phantom.

I trotted out, snagged the squeaker in my jaws, and slipped back into the rain. Another case closed. No parade, no medal—just a cold floor, a squeaky toy, and if the gods were merciful, three hours of uninterrupted sleep.
